Keynote — AI & Humanity

The Fork in the Road

MIT physicist Max Tegmark sees two paths out of this moment — a race to replace us, and a pro-human one. His argument: we can keep almost all of AI's upside without building the thing nobody knows how to control.

Max TegmarkMIT · Future of Life Institute

Tegmark opened with a show of hands. Who wants AI tools that cure diseases and solve hard problems? Every hand in the room. Who's excited for a future that makes them economically obsolete? Not one. Yet for years, he argued, we've been drifting down exactly that second path — the "race to replace," where machines take over more and more decisions, jobs, and even relationships.

01The tide is turning

The optimism in the talk was new. In the US, Republicans and Democrats hammered out 32 shared principles for pro-human AI; polls show only about 5% of Americans back an unregulated race to superintelligence. The Pope weighed in by encyclical. The White House has floated regulating AI like medicine, and even US–China talk of shared guardrails. The EU AI Act comes into full force; China now requires AI content to be labelled. "I've never seen things shift this quickly," he said, "in over a decade of working on this."

02Don't assume it's far, controllable, or necessary

People once called superintelligence decades away because AI "used to suck" — but image models and robots have gone from embarrassing to kung-fu in a few short years, on investment larger than the Manhattan Project. On control, he is stark: we still have no idea how to keep billions of machines vastly smarter than us in check. Turing said as much in 1951 — the smarter species tends to end up in charge. Walk to the zoo and ask who's in the cages.

"The greatest threat to any country is not another country. It's out-of-control machines."

03Tool AI, without AGI

Crucially, the upside doesn't require the dangerous part. Human capability has three superpowers — domain skill, generality, and autonomy — and an AI with only one or two can never wholesale replace you. Stay out of the white centre where all three combine, and humanity stays relevant and in charge. Tool AI alone can cut a million road deaths a year, eliminate hospital errors, diagnose cancers, fold proteins, accelerate research, personalise education — and even hit the UN's Sustainable Development Goals faster.

04And it's not inevitable

"People will build anything they can make money on" is simply false, he argued — we could profit from human cloning and chose not to. The mechanism is stigmatise, then regulate. His favourite jab: in the US today there are fewer rules on AI companies than on sandwich shops. A health inspector can shut your deli over 18 rats in the kitchen — but couldn't stop you shipping uncontrollable superintelligence at 8pm. Treat AI like any other industry, with an FDA-style "where's your clinical trial?", and the incentives flip.

05His big bet: provably-correct code

The talk closed on what Tegmark is most excited about — using AI to write code that is provably bug-free and unhackable. Formal verification has existed since Turing, but cost 10–100× more effort, so it never caught on. Now AI can write the proof too: success went from 68% to 96% in two years. His goal — "vari-coding" — is to write only the specification and let the AI produce both the code and a proof that it meets the spec.

The reason it works is asymmetry: discovering correct code and its proof is hard, but checking a proof is easy — a 300-line program on your laptop. So you can use a powerful AI you don't fully trust, and still be certain the result does exactly what you asked. No security patches, because there were never any bugs to begin with.

"It's much harder to find a needle in a haystack than to verify it's a needle once you've found it."

His last slide: we're at a fork, we've spent too long on the wrong path — but the pendulum is swinging. Win the pro-human future by treating AI with common-sense safety standards, so the industry is pushed to build controllable tools that do what we want, "not weird, creepy beings we have no idea how to control."
