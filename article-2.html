Panel — The Model Layer

Frontier, After the Benchmarks

Once topping a leaderboard stops being the point, what does "frontier" even mean? A frontier-models panel maps the next phase — from extraction to distribution, from scale to scope, and from closed APIs to the full stack.

Jeff SuMistral AI, APAC· CherryMiniMax· Hemant MohapatraLightspeed

There is a new frontier model out almost every day — but the panel's first move was to redefine what "frontier" means. Topping a benchmark is expensive, time-consuming, and the lead never lasts. What matters now, argued Mistral's Jeff Su, is frontier deployment: how much capability can you actually own, control and sustain in a volatile, geopolitical world where access to chips or APIs can be cut off?

MiniMax's Cherry framed it differently but arrived nearby: frontier is measured by how many people use your model every day — which makes accessibility and cost the real battlefield. Their M3 model, she noted, runs at roughly a fifth of the price of a leading Western model while handling serious coding and agentic work.

01From extraction to distribution

Lightspeed's Hemant Mohapatra offered the frame of the day. Every super-cycle — oil a century ago, CPU cycles, then GPU cycles, now AI tokens — begins in an extractive phase, where value accrues to whoever is best at pulling the new resource out of the ground. As the resource gets cheap and abundant, the cycle turns distributive: the prize is no longer extracting intelligence, but putting it to work on real, agentic problems.

"You're not in the oil-extraction business anymore. You're in the business of putting oil into machines and building better cars."

02Scale problems vs scope problems

His second lens: some problems are about scale — solve them once, then serve them cheaply a trillion times (coding is the canonical example). Others are about scope, where the terrain keeps changing as you go deeper: cancer, biology, materials science, mathematics. As the baseline models commoditise, the durable value — and the investable edge — moves into the scope problems.

03Europe's case as the third pole

Mistral's advantage, Su argued, is access to European industrial heavyweights — partnerships with ASML and Airbus bring vast proprietary data to bear on exactly those scope problems. The drag is structural: a real gap in funding and liquidity versus Silicon Valley, and a continent that is "27 different markets trying to operate as one." Still, his pitch was geopolitical: organisations shouldn't assume a two-horse race between the US and China.

04Open vs closed — and who actually gets paid

On open weights, Mohapatra was blunt. Philosophically, commoditising intelligence is good — it spreads the benefits — and open source is the fastest route there. But commodity doesn't mean cheap; it means fungible. The business-model trap: you can only monetise a token-metered model if you own the full stack beneath it — GPUs, energy, land. "You can't just sell water from a pond." MiniMax's answer is to ship both: open weights for local, privacy-bound deployments across APAC, and cloud-hosted closed models for day-one frontier access.

"Commoditisation doesn't mean it's cheap. It means it's fungible — intelligence from model one is no different from model two."

The closing analogies stuck: Anthropic and OpenAI as the Rolls-Royce, the challengers as the Mercedes most people actually buy; or the general model as a Swiss-army knife you prototype with, before swapping in specialist tools — wrapped in your own enterprise context — to build a real moat.
