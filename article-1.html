Keynote — The Economics of AI

Are the Models Just Infrastructure?

A trillion dollars of capital is pouring into AI. Benedict Evans argues the models themselves may end up looking a lot like railways and electricity — essential, expensive, and not where the money is made.

Benedict EvansIndependent Analyst· 21 min on stage

The biggest companies in tech have decided the risk of missing AI is bigger than the risk of over-investing — and they are spending accordingly. This year the hyperscalers will lay out something like $700 billion in capital expenditure, up from about $400 billion last year, with roughly a third of it dropping straight through to Nvidia. That is bigger than the telecom industry, and rivals oil and gas.

But the cash is starting to meet what Evans calls financial gravity. Meta, Microsoft and Alphabet are pushing past 50% of revenue into capex; Alphabet just raised $85 billion in equity, and Meta reportedly weighed the same. OpenAI, lacking that cash flow, is trying to "join the club" through SPVs, debt and a great deal of circular revenue.

01The bottleneck isn't money — it's physics

It turns out to be genuinely hard to spend this much, this fast. GPUs and memory are back-ordered; power, gas turbines and grid equipment are spoken for years out; and a political backlash against data centres is building in the US. Even Microsoft admits it has been "impossible to build this capacity fast enough."

The engine behind the surge is agentic coding. Anthropic has reported an unprecedented jump in run-rate; Uber burned its entire annual AI budget in the first quarter. Demand is racing far ahead of supply — even as inference efficiency improves 50 to 200× a year, and the next frontier model lands every six to nine months.

02The uncomfortable part: models look like commodities

Here is the thesis that should worry every lab. The frontier models are built with the same data, the same chips and the same infrastructure. There is no network effect and no fundamental reason any one of them holds a durable lead. Sam Altman imagines "intelligence as a utility, like electricity" — but mobile data grew up to two-thousand-fold over fifteen years while the carriers' stocks went nowhere. Commodities without network effects don't earn high margins.

"There's no fundamental reason why any of these companies is going to have a sustainable lead over the other."

03Chat is a bad interface — and usage is shallow

Adoption is "a mile wide and an inch deep." Even among tech workers, more people use these tools weekly or monthly than daily. Evans reaches for the spreadsheet: when Visicalc landed on the Apple II, it was life-changing for an accountant and a curiosity for a lawyer. Most people are tool-users, not tool-builders — someone has to wrap the raw capability in software that tells them what to do with it.

04So the value moves up the stack

If the models are infrastructure — very large, very expensive infrastructure — then the innovation moves into deployment. History rhymes: barcodes were meant to speed up checkout, but the by-product was accurate inventory, and that produced Walmart. We spent a century automating accounting and ended up with more accountants. AI gives you "infinite interns," but the real prize is the questions you could never ask before — listen to every customer call and tell me how to fix our churn.

Evans's closing posture is humility. We are early, everything is unsettled, and the honest answer to most AI questions is either "no one knows," or "what happened the last time everything changed?"

"Every question I get about AI has one of two answers: no one knows — or, what happened the last time that everything changed?"
